\begin{abstract}
Positive row scaling preserves a mixed-integer linear program in exact arithmetic, but
an observed numerical improvement need not identify the information used to select the
factors. We analyze a local--block--coupling construction and prove that geometric-mean
row centering can erase its block-scale information exactly. With a near-identity band,
the remaining scale information is bounded independently of the original heterogeneity.
A matched kernel control explains an apparent order-of-magnitude spectral improvement
without using block scales. We then characterize the positive row factors compatible
with an explicit original-unit residual budget and a coefficient window. The admissible
set is an interval; constrained geometric-mean centering is its logarithmic minimax
solution, and an empty interval provides an explicit incompatibility certificate.
An integer-rounding bound isolates an error that scaling cannot suppress, and a discrete
characterization selects exactly representable power-of-two exports.
A reconstruction of 240 dispatch runs separates completed-run effort from failures,
and 3,456 configurations of exactly enumerable binary stress instances distinguish
conditional row guarantees from solver behavior. Budget projection improves verification
on HiGHS in this stress experiment, but does not establish a solver-independent advantage;
a rounding-aware contract trades acceptance for conservative abstention. The results
provide precise controls for attributing scaling gains and for stating what a numerical
scaling policy can actually guarantee.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} mixed-integer linear programming; row scaling; numerical
reliability; equilibration; residual budgets; computational reproducibility.

\section{Introduction}
Model generators retain variable ownership, constraint roles and application units
that a conventional matrix export may discard. Such information can guide scaling,
but its presence in an algorithm does not establish that it affects the exported
matrix. Row coverage, normalization kernels, safeguards and metadata can change
together, confounding the interpretation of a numerical improvement.

In mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), positive row scaling preserves the
exact feasible set while changing the representation seen by presolve, relaxation
solves and integer acceptance tests. Coefficient magnitudes, matrix condition numbers,
original-data residuals and running time are therefore distinct outcomes. We study
which generator information remains identifiable after preprocessing and which
numerical guarantees can be stated independently of solver behavior.

First, we analyze a local--block--coupling construction whose block statistic is the
geometric mean of the smallest and largest post-local coefficient magnitudes. Exact
geometric-mean row centering makes every such statistic one; an identity band permits
only bounded variation. A matched Euclidean coupling-row control then explains an
apparent block-metadata benefit. Counterexamples and direct export comparisons delimit
the result when practical safeguards intervene.

Second, an original-unit residual budget supplies information that survives a change
of row units when transformed with the row. We characterize the factors that combine
this budget with coefficient limits, derive the constrained logarithmic minimax
factor, and extend admissibility to integer rounding and exact power-of-two export.
An empty admissible set certifies incompatible numerical specifications, not MILP
infeasibility. Solver acceptance remains a separate question.

The evidence combines a controlled matrix audit, reconstructed dispatch measurements,
and exactly enumerable binary stress instances. Direct tolerance scaling and matched
kernel controls prevent crediting the proposed construction with benefits already
explained by simpler rules. The contribution is a characterization and reproducible
audit of these mechanisms, with explicit limits on the guarantees.

\section{Model, policies and comparisons}
\label{sec:model}
Consider
\[
 \min c^Tx\quad\text{subject to }Ax\le b,\quad l\le x\le u,\quad x_j\in\mathbb Z\ (j\in J).
\]
Equalities and opposite inequality senses are allowed. For a positive diagonal matrix
$D_r$, replacing $(A,b)$ by $(D_rA,D_rb)$ preserves every constraint, variable domain
and objective value in exact arithmetic. It is essential to multiply the whole row,
including its right-hand side, by the same positive number. Dividing different
coefficients of a coupling row by different block scales is not a row scaling and is
outside this equivalence statement.

Let the generator provide a fixed partition of the local rows into nonempty blocks,
a coupling-row set, and a column-to-block map $\beta$. Coverage specifies which rows
are eligible for scaling; a kernel maps eligible row data to a candidate factor;
safeguards decide which factors are applied. These are separate choices. A block map
can influence the factors even when coverage and kernels are fixed, but only if the
block summaries retain information and are actually used.

For a nonzero row, put $m_r=\min_{a_{rj}\ne0}|a_{rj}|$ and
$M_r=\max_j|a_{rj}|$. The geometric-mean (GM) factor is $(m_rM_r)^{-1/2}$;
the Euclidean (L2) factor is $1/\|a_r\|_2$. Both are coefficient-only rules.
Calling them ``the same kernel'' because neither uses the RHS is incorrect.
The following comparisons keep this distinction visible.
\begin{table}[tb]
\centering\small
\caption{Controls for the coefficient-only construction. Row eligibility and safeguards
must also be matched for the indicated comparison. A role map and a block-scale map are
separate information sources.}\label{tab:policies}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{lXXX}
\toprule
Policy & Local rows & Coupling rows & Information used\\
\midrule
Base & None & None & None\\
Flat-GM & GM & GM & Row coefficients\\
Flat-L2 & L2 & L2 & Row coefficients\\
Role-Hybrid & GM & L2 & Row roles, coefficients\\
SA-Mat & GM & $\left[\sum_j(a_{rj}/s_{\beta(j)})^2\right]^{-1/2}$ & Roles, block scales, ownership\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The direct test of the \emph{block scales} is SA-Mat versus Role-Hybrid at matched
coverage and safeguards. Flat-L2 also changes the local kernel, whereas Flat-GM changes
the coupling kernel. Relabeling blocks without changing their assignments is an
invariance check. Permuting the assignment of unequal scales is a different intervention:
it tests the usefulness of the map on the chosen instances, and is not assumed to
preserve semantic validity.

\section{Computational design}
\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Matrix attribution audit}
The C++ generator constructs local blocks and coupling rows while retaining their row
roles and column ownership. The archived headline cell uses four heterogeneous blocks,
coefficient exponent range $[-2,2]$, coupling disturbance severity $6$, and 30 seeds.
All policies in \cref{tab:policies} are available in the same implementation. Their
exported matrices allow a comparison before any optimizer is called. The analysis
records spectral pseudo-condition numbers and direct export comparisons; equal
condition numbers alone are not evidence of identical matrices.

The spectral diagnostic is $\kappa_2^+(A)=\sigma_{\max}(A)/\sigma_{\min}^+(A)$,
where numerical rank is determined relative to the largest singular value. It is a
diagnostic of the exported constraint matrix, not of the active basis at every node.
Variable bounds, redundant rows and slack conventions can change its interpretation.
A condition-number ratio is never used as a proxy for verified solver success.

\subsection{Operational reconstruction}
The operational case study uses the final four-arm campaign from the dispatch
repository: Base, Flat-GM, Flat-L2 and Role-Hybrid; 15 instances from each of the
Simple and SG-Ter-Mer families; Gurobi; relative MIP gaps 1\% and 0.1\%; seed 42;
16 threads; and a 1,800-second solver limit. The fixed subsets are retained rather
than reselected after inspecting outcomes. This gives 240 assigned runs. We retrieved
all native logs from the cluster and preserved input hashes.

The primary runtime endpoint is arithmetic mean PAR10 over all 15 assigned instances
per cell, with 18,000 seconds assigned to a non-completion. This measures solver
completion rather than independently certified feasibility. Native logs recover Work
at a common resolution of 0.01 units; the archived runner had accidentally rounded
scaled-variant markers to integers. Work is analyzed separately on the common
all-variants-completed pool, with exploratory paired bootstrap intervals. Online
Resource 1 specifies the reconstruction, resolution sensitivity and residual checks.
The selected pools and repeated instances across gap settings do not constitute
independent replications of general solver performance.

\subsection{Enumerated residual-budget stress experiment}
We construct three binary families with 12 variables: a cardinality row, three positive
packing rows, and three rows with signed integer coefficients. Profits are positive
integers. If $W$ is the integer matrix and $k$ an integer capacity vector, the canonical
constraints are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stress-family}
 10^e Wx\le 10^e k-0.1\,\boldsymbol 1,\qquad x\in\{0,1\}^{12},
 \quad e\in\{0,3,6,9\}.
\end{equation}
For these exponents the exact binary feasible set is $Wx\le k-\boldsymbol 1$.
Thus changing $e$ changes the numerical representation and continuous relaxation while
preserving the binary feasible set. It is not pure row re-emission, because the absolute
margin 0.1 remains fixed. The corresponding distinction is intentional: it exposes
integer acceptance near a facet without changing the binary optimum.

The budget is 0.01 in the canonical row's units. These are declared stress-test units,
not engineering tolerances inferred from the dispatch data. Eight seeds, 100--107, were
reserved after a one-seed exploratory pilot; all four exponents and all three families
are retained. Both presolve settings are run on HiGHS 1.15.1 and Gurobi 13.0.2, with one
thread, zero target MIP gap, a ten-second limit, and primal/integrality tolerances
$10^{-6}$. The nine policies give 3,456 planned configurations. Budget-round abstains
on 288 of them, leaving 3,168 optimizer calls.

Base, GM and L2 have their definitions in \cref{tab:policies}, with full coverage and no
identity-band veto in this experiment. GM-tight uses GM and tightens both tolerance
settings to $10^{-9}$. Budget-only uses the established direct factor
$\epsilon/\delta_r$. Budget-GM uses \cref{prop:budget}, coefficient limits
$[10^{-9},10^9]$ and $\epsilon=10^{-6}$. Budget-round additionally reserves
$E_r=10^{-6}\sum_j|a_{rj}|$ for rounding and abstains on the whole model if any row has
an empty interval. Abstention is counted separately, not as a successful solve.
Dyadic-GM uses a power-of-two GM factor with coefficient limits and an exact
binary64 export; Budget-dyadic additionally imposes the row budget through
Proposition~\ref{prop:dyadic}. These two arms were added after inspecting the
floating-point endpoint issue in the initial run. They are an explicitly exploratory
arithmetic sensitivity, not an independent holdout. The original seven arms are retained
with the corrected endpoint implementation and unchanged instance seeds.

All $2^{12}$ assignments are enumerated to obtain the binary optimum. The script checks
that each stored binary64 RHS lies strictly between the two adjacent scaled integer
activities used in that argument. Returned solutions, row factors and generated data
are saved. Original row residuals are evaluated as exact rational expressions in the
stored binary64 data. A verified optimum requires a feasible rounded binary assignment
with the enumerated objective, valid original variable bounds, and a returned point
within $10^{-9}$ of its rounded assignment. We also record residual budgets and
integrality errors separately. This conservative acceptance rule is declared independently
of the solver's status; a rejected approximately integral point is not automatically a
solver implementation error.

This experiment tests a failure mechanism. It is too small and deliberately too close
to integer feasibility boundaries to estimate practical runtime or failure rates on
industrial MILPs. Counts are descriptive, and no significance tests treat the repeated
solver settings on a generated instance as independent samples.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{A kernel effect mistaken for a block effect}
In the archived heterogeneous headline cell, the median spectral diagnostic is
$5.30\times10^8$ for Base, $1.36\times10^4$ for Flat-GM,
$1.33\times10^3$ for SA-Mat and $4.68\times10^2$ for Flat-L2.
The ratio of Flat-GM to SA-Mat is about 10.2. That comparison changes the coupling
kernel. Role-Hybrid matches SA-Mat in the 30 archived spectral records, and permuting
the block-scale assignments leaves the same diagnostic. The collapse result explains
why the block-relative formula becomes an ordinary L2 normalization in this regime.
Flat-L2's smaller diagnostic does not establish a better MIP policy: it changes local
normalization as well and requires a separate solve/verification evaluation.

Using pre-local block scales does not rescue a positive metadata claim on these
instances. SA-Pre is worse than Role-Hybrid on all 30 archived instances, with a median
paired condition-number ratio about 4.27. In the exhaustive 24-permutation assignment
sweep, the true scale assignment is never the best; its median rank is 18. These are
falsification results for this choice of summary and benchmark. They are not a theorem
that semantic metadata or all block-based preconditioners are useless.

\subsection{Completed-run gains and timeout-sensitive outcomes}

On the 14 common completed SG-Ter-Mer instances, Flat-GM/Base has Work
geometric-mean ratios 0.698 and 0.682 at the two gaps. Yet instance077 completes
under Base and times out under every scaled policy at both gaps, sharply reversing
the fixed-pool PAR10 ordering (Online Resource 1, Table S1). The Simple family also has
no uniform ordering: Flat-GM reduces the arithmetic runtime summary at 1\% but
increases it at 0.1\%. These results illustrate why completed-run effort and
failure-sensitive runtime must be reported separately.

Original-data residuals also require an explicit interpretation. An absolute equality
residual of 4.97 on a zero-RHS row in caso46e is not a 497\% physical error;
the same run's maximum activity-normalized residual is $1.15\times10^{-7}$.
Online Resource 1 gives both definitions and the exploratory Work intervals.
Neither residual normalization establishes application acceptance without a justified
original-unit budget.

The fixed margin in \eqref{eq:stress-family} makes the continuous feasibility boundary

The full counts appear in Online Resource 1 (Table S2). Budget-GM verifies 182 of 192 configurations on HiGHS, compared with 141 for GM and
167 for Base. Budget-only verifies 191: direct budget scaling is more successful than the extra
minimax centering in this experiment. On Gurobi,
Budget-GM verifies 152, compared with 140 for GM and 156 for Base. GM-tight verifies
166 under each solver. Thus the row-budget construction does not establish a
solver-independent reliability improvement, and its conditional hypothesis must remain
visible.

Dyadic-GM verifies 142 configurations on HiGHS and 140 on Gurobi;
Budget-dyadic verifies 161 and 153, respectively. Exact representation of the scaled
row is therefore insufficient for reliable solver acceptance on these stress cases.
In a preserved earlier implementation, rounding the budget endpoint to nearest instead
of inward changed HiGHS Budget-GM's verified count from 182 to 191. This is a numerical
sensitivity result, not a reason to prefer a factor that fails its declared interval.
The artifact preserves both implementations' outputs and identifies the corrected run.

Budget-round accepts and verifies 48 configurations per solver, and abstains on the
other 144. Its zero observed verification failures have a substantial cost: it declines
many instances that another policy solves correctly. This is expected of a worst-case
rounding bound. An incompatibility certificate explains why the requested uniform
contract cannot be asserted; it is not evidence that the MILP itself cannot be solved.
Figure~\ref{fig:budget-verification} reports the denominator explicitly so that abstention
cannot masquerade as perfect overall performance.



\section{Discussion and limits}
\label{sec:discussion}
The collapse theorem concerns extrema-based summaries after geometric-mean
normalization. It does not rule out useful variable scales, physical tolerances or
other block statistics. Its practical implication is to hold coverage, kernels and
safeguards fixed before attributing an effect to block information, and to inspect
the actual row factors or named exports. Safeguards can introduce discontinuities;
equal scalar diagnostics or rounded Work values cannot prove export identity.

Residual budgets give a distinct interpretation to generator information. Their joint
compatibility with coefficient limits is explicit and invariant under consistent
row re-emission. The contract remains conditional: integer-rounding amplification
and internal solver transformations can defeat predictions based on the exported
row alone. Production use requires a postsolve acceptance or repair policy and
measurement of its full cost.

The dispatch reconstruction covers two application classes, one solver, one seed and
15 selected instances per class. The new budget policies have not been validated
there using application-supplied engineering tolerances. The matrix audit tests an
algebraic mechanism; the binary instances deliberately amplify tolerance sensitivity.
Neither estimates typical industrial failure rates or speedups. Enumeration certifies
the small-instance optima, with no corresponding exact optimality claim for dispatch.

\section{Conclusion}
For the studied local--block--coupling construction, geometric-mean row centering
can remove all information from a subsequent extrema-based block-scale summary.
A bounded identity band only restores bounded scale variation. This explains why
changing a coupling-row kernel can look like a block-metadata benefit when the
comparison does not hold kernels fixed. The operational reconstruction further shows
that favorable completed-run effort can coexist with an unfavorable failure-sensitive
runtime summary.

An explicit residual budget provides a separate, interpretable basis for row scaling.
Its compatibility with coefficient limits can be characterized exactly, and constrained
logarithmic centering has a closed-form solution. Integer-rounding amplification and
solver-internal transformations limit what this conditional contract can guarantee.
The resulting recommendation is to expose the information used, audit the actual
transformation, declare incompatible specifications, and verify the returned point.
